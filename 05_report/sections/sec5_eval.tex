\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Workload Profile}
The workload splits along the lines argued in Section~\ref{sec:context}: dispatch and payment writes need strong consistency, while reporting queries can tolerate weaker freshness. Q6 (hourly dispatch), Q1 (daily reconciliation), Q5 (periodic collections), Q2 (collections by driver) and Q7 (monthly reporting) are the five most frequent queries. All ten queries in Section~\ref{sec:sql} are read-only SELECTs; writes go to \texttt{Trip} and \texttt{Payment} at dispatch and settlement time.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Index Before/After}
We picked a query that counts completed trips since 2026-10, combining a \texttt{status} equality check with a \texttt{start\_time} range condition. With no secondary index on those columns the plan is a full table scan (\texttt{type=ALL}, rows $\approx$60{,}261). After building a composite index on \texttt{(status, start\_time)} --- the equality column first, the range column second, following the leftmost-prefix rule --- the plan switches to an index range scan. The leaves already contain everything the query needs, so there is no table lookup at all (\texttt{Using index}).

The same session also showed the non-sargable trap. Wrapping the indexed column in a function (\texttt{DATE(start\_time) = '2026-10-15'}) forced a sequential scan of the whole index, while writing the condition as a half-open interval brought \texttt{type} back to \texttt{range}. Against the latency target G2 (P95 $<$ 200 ms), the indexed plan sits two orders of magnitude below the bar.

\subsection{Experiment 2: JOIN vs Subquery}
``Completed trips without payment'' (Q2 and Q5) can be written three ways: \texttt{NOT EXISTS}, \texttt{NOT IN}, and \texttt{LEFT JOIN ... IS NULL}. All three returned the same 1{,}442 rows on our data, and the plans came out identical: a full scan of \texttt{Trip}, each row probed against the unique index on \texttt{Payment.trip\_id} (\texttt{eq\_ref}), with \texttt{Extra: Not exists; Using index} --- the optimizer had rewritten both subquery forms into the same anti-join as the explicit LEFT JOIN version.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Three anti-join formulations on the same question.}
\label{tab:exp2}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Formulation & result & plan signature & actual time \\
\midrule
\texttt{NOT EXISTS}          & 1{,}442 & \texttt{Not exists; Using index} & 89 ms \\
\texttt{NOT IN}              & 1{,}442 & same as above & 82 ms \\
\texttt{LEFT JOIN ... IS NULL} & 1{,}442 & same type, minor \texttt{filtered} diff & 90 ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\texttt{NOT IN} carries a caveat: the subquery column has to be NOT NULL for it to work correctly. \texttt{Payment.trip\_id} is, so all three are safe here; but if it allowed NULL the whole result would silently come back empty. That is the practical reason to check EXPLAIN before committing to one formulation (see Q4 in Section~\ref{sec:sql}).

\subsection{Experiment 3: Concurrent Transactions}
We used two Workbench windows connected to the same database, operating on row \texttt{trip\_id=92} of \texttt{trip\_detail} (initial rating 3).

In the first scenario both sessions read the rating into their own variables, then wrote back computed values. Session A stored 3, added 1, and committed a 4. Session B, still holding its copy of 3, subtracted 1 and committed a 2 --- overwriting A's 4. Both UPDATE statements reported ``1 row(s) affected'', yet A's increment was gone.

The second scenario replayed the same steps, except A locked the row with \texttt{SELECT ... FOR UPDATE} before writing. B's identical \texttt{FOR UPDATE} statement now blocked until A committed. Once unblocked, B read the committed value (3, not the stale copy) and added 1 again. Both increments survived: $2 \to 3 \to 4$.

For the third scenario we switched to row \texttt{trip\_id=150}. Under the default REPEATABLE READ, a transaction that read the rating as 3 kept seeing 3 even after another session committed a 5 --- the snapshot does not update mid-transaction. Under READ COMMITTED the same sequence saw the committed 5, then saw the 3 after a second commit. This is exactly why plain SELECTs were not enough in scenario 2, and why \texttt{FOR UPDATE} was needed there.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Concurrency scenarios and constraint interceptions.}
\label{tab:exp3}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Scenario & Observation & What it shows \\
\midrule
Lost update & final rating 2, one write lost & the failure mode \\
\texttt{FOR UPDATE} & B waits, then $2\to3\to4$ & row lock fixes it \\

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The last three rows also serve as evidence for G5: the constraints themselves stop illegal writes at the schema layer, without any application code involved.

\subsection{Distribution Check against the RideAustin Holdout}
The generator parameters came from the 80\% training split of RideAustin; we then compared the generated 60{,}000 trips against the held-out 20\%.

Status mix, payment methods and the unpaid rate follow course-defined rules with no counterpart in the source data, so we left them out of the table.

\subsection{Results Summary and Goal Recheck}
The composite index brought estimated rows down from 60{,}261 to 3{,}259 and actual latency from 16.6 ms to 0.46 ms, roughly 36 times faster and well inside the 200 ms P95 target. The three anti-join formulations produced identical results and near-identical plans, confirming that the optimizer, not the syntax, decides performance. On concurrency, the lost update turned out to be a read-modify-write problem that \texttt{FOR UPDATE} solved; the three error codes (1452, 1062, 3819) showed the schema constraints working on their own. These results, together with the holdout check, address the measurable goals set in Section~\ref{sec:context}.
