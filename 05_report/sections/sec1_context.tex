\subsection{Business Context}
\label{subsec:background}

This project builds a relational database for a ride-hailing platform to support trip queries, payment reconciliation and operational reporting. It retains five base entities, \texttt{Passenger}, \texttt{Driver}, \texttt{Vehicle}, \texttt{Trip} and \texttt{Payment}, and adds \texttt{trip\_detail}, \texttt{service\_tier} and \texttt{pickup\_zone}. The extension tables associate service tiers, pickup zones and optional ratings with trips, giving eight entities in total.

Foreign key and unique constraints ensure valid relationships between records. Each driver is assigned one vehicle, each vehicle has at most one driver, and each trip has at most one payment. The generator also creates one detail record for every trip.

The recorded workflow begins after successful dispatch, when the driver accepts the request. Unmatched requests therefore fall outside the database scope. Trip status progresses through \texttt{accepted}, \texttt{en\_route} and \texttt{completed}. Early termination after boarding is classified as completed. Before boarding, cancellation may be free or charged. The course-defined cancellation fee is USD~1.50, subject to a waiver for excessive pickup delays. Actual charges are recorded in \texttt{Payment}~\cite{ubercancelfee,uberengmysql}. Figure~\ref{fig:business-flow} in Appendix~\ref{app:screens} summarises the workflow.

\subsection{Stakeholder Requirements}
\label{subsec:stakeholders}

Passengers need trip histories and fare/payment reconciliation. Drivers need cumulative trip and fare totals and verifiable vehicle assignments. Operations staff require peak-hour trip volumes, cancellation rates and assignment information. Finance staff need unpaid-trip reconciliation and payment totals by method. Customer service requires complete trip records within 30 seconds to investigate disputed fares.

These requirements form the basis for the database relationships, integrity constraints and queries.

These are design targets. The Top-5 queries are Q6, Q1, Q5, Q2 and Q7. Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the available evidence, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} reviews their achievement. G2 requires repeated query measurements, while G3 requires load testing before throughput can be confirmed.

At sample scale, a single instance simplifies deployment and consistency management. The design prioritises consistent dispatch and payment records, with writes suspended during an instance outage. Failed multi-step transactions should roll back all their updates. Reporting may tolerate minute-level staleness as a design assumption, while customer-service queries require current data. Reports and writes share the same instance, so a future read replica could reduce resource contention. Availability and replica performance require further testing. Other constraints include MySQL 8.0.45 compatibility, rebuilding within 30 minutes using fixed-seed scripts, and 180-day online retention followed by archiving.

\subsection{Dataset Selection}
\label{subsec:dataset}

The project uses synthetic data because the public datasets considered do not provide all the identifiers, vehicle assignments and payment relationships required by the schema~\cite{tlctripdata}. Fixed-seed generation allows members to rebuild the same dataset and reproduce query results.

Generation parameters are based on full-dataset RideAustin statistics for service tiers, fares and ratings~\cite{zuniga2020rideaustin}. The comparison is therefore descriptive. Training-only calibration lacks independently verifiable evidence. Cancellations, payment methods, unpaid rates and pickup zones are course assumptions.

The final sample contains 60{,}000 trips spanning one year, supporting monthly reporting demonstrations. It intentionally includes 1{,}442 completed trips without payment records for reconciliation tests. The sample includes historical records, while the 180-day online retention and archival policy remains a design requirement.
